Lesson 8 · Last Layer

Positioning the Corners

A three-piece cycle that leaves everything else alone — and it turns out to be the famous commutator, one lesson early.

Cross done, cross edges placed (Lesson 7). Only the four yellow corners remain, and — like the edges — they split into position first, twist second. This lesson gets every corner into its correct home. It may end up twisted there; that's fine and expected. Twisting is Lesson 9's job.

Step 1 — find the corner that's already home

A corner is home when its three colors are the same three colors as the faces meeting at its spot — in any twist. Example: the corner between the yellow, red, and blue centers is home if it carries yellow-red-blue stickers, even if yellow points sideways. Check all four top corners:

  • All four home — skip to Lesson 9.
  • Exactly one home — the usual case. Hold the cube so that corner sits at front-right-top.
  • None home — apply the algorithm below once from any angle; exactly one corner will come home, guaranteed. Then proceed as above.

You'll never find exactly two or three home (with the edges already placed) — same flavor of impossibility as Lesson 7's "never exactly three edges."

Step 2 — cycle the other three

U R U'  ·  L'  ·  U R' U'  ·  L

The dots aren't moves — they mark the four chunks you're about to meet below. Read it 3 + 1 + 3 + 1.

With the home corner held at front-right-top, this cycles the other three corners around while the held one stays put: front-left jumps diagonally to back-right, back-right slides to back-left, and back-left comes forward to front-left. Apply it, check — if the three aren't home yet, apply it once more (the second pass runs the loop the remaining step; a 3-cycle always lands within two tries).

The big idea: this is a commutator Split the algorithm in half and look at the halves: U R U' · L' · U R' U' · L. Call the first chunk A and the second B — then the whole thing is A B A' B': do A, do B, undo A, undo B. That shape is called a commutator, and it's the deepest trick in cubing. Notation: [A, B] = A B A⁻¹ B⁻¹. And notice A = U R U' is itself a conjugate — Lesson 6's pattern, nested inside.
Why commutators disturb so little Here's the intuition, and it's worth a slow read. If A and B touched completely separate parts of the cube, A B A' B' would do nothing — each undo would perfectly cancel its do, like two people tidying different rooms. The only pieces that end up changed are the few living in the overlap, where B disturbs what A was counting on undoing. Choose A and B with a tiny overlap — here they share little more than a corner's path — and you get an algorithm that moves exactly three pieces and restores everything else automatically. Nobody memorized "eight moves that happen to work": the structure guarantees the cleanup.
Do this now Solve to the end of Lesson 7. Find your home corner (check colors, ignore twist), grip it at front-right-top, and run the cycle — slowly, watching the three corners chase each other around while the fourth sits still. Repeat if needed. Verify: all four corners home (colors match their three surrounding centers), twists still allowed to be wrong.

Stuck or curious? Verify the commutator idea with your own hands: pick an A and B that touch completely separate territory — say A = U, B = D — and do A B A' B'. Nothing changes, exactly as the overlap argument predicts; the three-corner cycle above works only because its A and B do overlap, slightly. If your three corners aren't home after two applications, the held corner wasn't truly home (check its colors against all three surrounding centers, ignoring twist) — re-find it and re-grip.
