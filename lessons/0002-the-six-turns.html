Lesson 2 · Foundations

Reading & Writing the Six Turns

Notation isn't bureaucracy — it's how an algorithm becomes a thing you can remember, share, and reverse.

In Lesson 1 you learned what moves: the 20 pieces. Now you need a precise way to say how you move them. Every algorithm you'll ever learn is just a short string in this language — so an hour spent getting fluent here pays off forever.

One letter per face

Hold your cube still and don't rotate the whole thing while reading a sequence. Each face gets a letter, named by where it is relative to you:

  • U — Up (top)
  • D — Down (bottom)
  • L — Left  ·  R — Right
  • F — Front (facing you)  ·  B — Back

A bare letter means: turn that face only, a quarter turn (90°), clockwise as you look directly at that face. This "look at the face" rule is the one people trip on — see the note below.

Why "as you look at it" matters R and L are both "clockwise," but since you view them from opposite sides, they spin in opposite real-world directions. The notation is consistent; your viewpoint is what flips. Always mentally step around to face the layer you're turning. The worst offender is B: viewed from behind the cube, its clockwise looks counter-clockwise from where you sit — and unlike L, you can't easily peek at it while turning. Double-check every B.
Do this now — cube in hand Hold the cube with one face toward you (that's F). Do R, then U, then F, watching only which layer moves each time. Don't rotate the whole cube between turns — keep it planted. Say each letter aloud as you do it. Then check yourself: undo it with F' U' R' — if the cube returns exactly to where it started, you read all six turns right. (That undo-in-reverse is the very rule you're about to learn.)

Two modifiers: prime and 2

R quarter turn clockwise
R' ("R prime") quarter turn counter-clockwise
R2 half turn (180°) — direction doesn't matter

The formal idea hiding here: a move is a function, prime is its inverse

This is worth seeing properly, because it's the engine behind every solve. Think of a turn as a function — it takes the cube in one state and returns it in another. R is a function; so is the whole sequence R U F.

Every such function has an inverse: the move that exactly undoes it. The inverse of R is R' — do one then the other and you're back where you started. In symbols, mathematicians write the inverse of R as R⁻¹, which is precisely what the prime means.

Why this is the whole game To invert a sequence, reverse the order and prime each move. The inverse of R U is U' R' — like taking off your shoes then socks: undo in reverse, each step reversed. Later, algorithms work by setting up a position, doing something, then running that setup in inverse to clean up. You can't do that without this idea. This "reverse and invert each step" is exactly how inverses of compositions work in group theory.
Do this now — prove the inverse to yourself Start from a solved cube (so the return is unmistakable) and do R U R' U' six times, counting reps aloud. Repeating it six times adds up to no change at all — mathematicians say the sequence has order 6 — so the cube comes back exactly. Watch the pieces cycle out and come home: proof that nothing is random; turns compose and reverse predictably.

Lock it in — from memory Name all six face letters. What's the difference between R' and R2? Write down the inverse of U F R without looking. (Answer: reverse + prime each.)

Stuck or curious? To drill inverses until they're automatic: write five random three-move sequences, invert each on paper (reverse + prime), then do sequence-plus-inverse on the cube — a solved cube must come back exactly, so every drill self-checks. If it doesn't return, the usual culprit is B (see the viewpoint note above). When you can read R U R' U' without translating, continue to Lesson 3: the first layer's cross, by reasoning not recipe.
