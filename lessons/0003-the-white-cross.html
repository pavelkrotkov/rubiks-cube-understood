Lesson 3 · First Layer

The White Cross, by Reasoning

Your first real solving step — and the proof that the cube is logic, not luck. No algorithm required.

Everything from Lesson 1 pays off now. Because you think in pieces with a position and an orientation, you can solve this entire step by pure thought. There's no sequence to memorize here — only a question to keep asking.

The goal, stated precisely

Put white on the bottom (so the white center faces down, yellow up). We want the four white edge pieces placed around the white center to form a cross on the bottom — but with one strict condition.

The condition most beginners ignore Each white edge has two colors — white plus one other (say white-blue). Its one true home is between the two centers of those colors. So a white-blue edge belongs touching the white center and the blue center. A cross where white lines up on the bottom but the side colors don't match the side centers is not solved — it just looks like a plus sign. Position and orientation, both.
See the trap with your own cube Find any white edge and place it so white points down. Now look at its side color and the center right above it on that side face. If they don't match, the piece is in the wrong home even though the bottom "looks" right. That mismatch is the whole reason the cross takes thought.

The reasoning question

For each white edge, ask: "Which two centers does this piece belong between?" The answer is fixed by the piece's own two colors. Then your job is just to get it there with white facing down. That's the entire step.

A reliable, reasoning-friendly technique: the daisy

Here's a clean way to do it that you can fully follow, not just copy:

  1. Make a daisy on top. With yellow on top, bring all four white edges up next to the yellow center so each shows white facing up — a yellow center ringed by four white "petals." Turn faces to ferry white edges to the top; don't worry about side colors yet. One rule keeps you safe: before turning any face to raise a new petal, first turn U so no petal you've already made is sitting above that face — otherwise the turn knocks it back off. This stages all four pieces where you can see and aim them.
  2. Aim one petal. Pick a white petal. Turn the top (U) until that petal's other color matches the center of the side face directly below it (e.g. the blue side color sits above the blue center).
  3. Drop it down. Turn that whole side face 180° (e.g. F2). The white edge swings straight down to the bottom, into its true home, white facing down.
  4. Repeat for the other three petals. Aim with U, drop with a double turn of that side face (F2, R2, B2, or L2). Done — a correct white cross.
Why the 180° drop is safe A half-turn of a side face carries the top edge to the bottom on the same face — so the side color stays matched to its center, and white ends up down. Because you aimed first, the piece lands both correctly positioned and correctly oriented in one move. That's reasoning doing the work an algorithm would otherwise do.
Do the whole thing now Scramble, then solve the white cross with the daisy method. When you think it's done, keep white on the bottom and run the verification below before trusting it.

How to verify (don't skip)

Hold white on the bottom and look at the four side faces. On each side you should see a matching two-sticker column: the center, and directly below it the edge's side sticker in the same color. (Tilt the cube to peek underneath and confirm the white plus-sign too.) If all four sides match, your cross is genuinely solved — not just plus-shaped. A full matching "T" on each side arrives in Lesson 4, once the corners join the row.

Stuck or curious? If a new petal keeps knocking an old one off, you're skipping the shelter rule — turn U first so no finished petal sits above the face you're about to turn. If an edge shows white sideways in the top layer, one turn of its side face reorients it for the daisy. And why white first? No deep reason — any color works; white-then-yellow is just the convention most guides (and this course) share, which makes other resources easier to follow.
